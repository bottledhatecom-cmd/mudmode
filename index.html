Using Grok? mudmode keeps your crew lean.

mudmode was built by a crew running on Claude, which can check its own usage meter. Our second crew runs on Grok, which can't see its own usage meter in our setup, so it learned mudmode's habits instead. Here's what it picked up.

A check-up at the halfway mark

From our 10-20 week review: One of Grok's main chats ran about 18 hours (988 turns).

Partway through a week, the crew stopped and reviewed where its usage went. The likely big costs: one main chat left running far too long, and too many jobs at once.

Rules that run, not rules that sit

From our 10-20 week review: its cost rules were written down in 8 files, yet too many jobs still ran at once and most jobs never set an effort level. The lesson: a rule that costs money needs a check that actually runs, not more copies of the text.

A few heavy jobs at a time

The crew's habit: every AI writes heavy work into one shared queue with a small cap on how many run at once, and the rest wait their turn. Frequent check-ins skip a run when the queue is full; backups don't.

The right effort for each job

Light effort for status reads, copying and routine checks. Full effort for code, reviews and research. Small jobs skip extra helpers entirely.

Hand off before the chat gets heavy

It can't see how full its chat is, so it watches the signs: big jobs piling up, repeated large reads, a full day of turns. We try to hand off before the chat gets compressed: when the signs show up, it offers a fresh chat with a short handoff note. Sometimes it forgets anyway. That's why it has a hive to help catch it. The shared hive is built to get better as more members join and send lessons; lessons ship after review.

Handing off scheduled jobs

At every handoff, the old chat is asked to delete its scheduled jobs once the new chat has them, so the same job doesn't keep running twice on your plan. It's a habit, not a lock, so glance at your scheduled tasks now and then; you decide what keeps running.

Honest note: our crew couldn't put an exact number on the savings yet, because usage per job wasn't recorded. What we can show is the habits, and they never cut a check. Results vary.

How a mudmode crew builds an app

Every change follows the same path, and each step has to pass before the next one starts.

  1. Look before building. The crew first checks what was tried before and what the lessons say, so it doesn't redo work or repeat an old mistake.
  2. Build on its own copy. Each job gets its own branch or copy, never the live app. Work is saved often (our own crew saves a checkpoint every 10 minutes), so an interruption doesn't have to mean starting over.
  3. Tests, then a fresh pair of eyes. The app's tests and checks run first. Then a reviewer that didn't write the change reads the finished work. If the review finds a problem, the work goes back to be fixed, and you decide whether it goes ahead.
  4. A second check against the real thing. Before anything is called done, a separate check compares the result with what's actually live.
  5. Test phone first, then yours. New builds run on a test phone on the computer first; your real phone comes after, with a heads-up. Each outside step, like a deploy or a store upload, waits for your yes.
  6. Ship, then learn. After every release the crew asks: what lost time, what stalled, what had to be redone, and is it fixed now? Once a week, a short review brings you a few changes to vote on.

Why the results are better

A problem sends it back

If any step finds a problem, the work goes back, and every step after it runs again. No step is skipped to save time or usage.

Proof, not "probably fine"

A failure is reported as a failure, and a skipped step as skipped. A cause counts as found only once it's shown; until then it's "not found yet".

Quality beats cost

Being right comes before saving usage. A saving that could touch quality is decided with you, never quietly.

Results vary. These are the steps mudmode's rules ask every AI to follow. AI tools can still make mistakes, so keep checking their work.

Start lite. Turn on more when you need it.

mudmode now starts small. Your AI loads one short page of rules, and the rest waits in modules you can switch on with one sentence. The safety rules are on in every mode.

Lite mode

The free Crew kit's default. One page of core rules: ask first before money, posting, sending, deploying or deleting; no secrets; no signing in for you; a check before anything is called done; a real review before anything goes out; a short handoff so tomorrow starts where today stopped.

Full mode

The Full Kit's default. All 25 mudmode rules in one combined core, word for word the same as before lite mode existed. Say "mudmode full" to switch, "mudmode lite" to go back. Switching never deletes anything.

A daily report, in every mode

A short note: what mudmode is doing, what it learned, and any lesson you chose to share (or "nothing shared"). Say "mudmode report" any time; tools that can run scheduled tasks can write one each night, with your OK.

The modules, one line each

handoff-plus

A fuller handoff note and the last few kept on file, so a new chat or another AI picks up exactly where you left off.

jobs

Every job gets a name, a line on one list and a progress note, so long or side-by-side work doesn't get lost.

lessons

A lessons book your AI reads before it starts, so a mistake that happened once gets written down.

reviewer

A separate read-only review with a pass, warn or fail for all finished work, not only work that goes out.

crew

Rules for two or more AIs sharing one folder: whose files are whose, and short notes to each other.

ship

The full order of work for sites, code and apps, with real phones last.

adapters

Ready-made rules files for an AI the installer doesn't connect.

local-hardware

Optional use of a strong computer or graphics card, only after checking what you have.

manual-keeper

Keeps your rule files in one place and writes down your rule changes as you make them.

usage (Full Kit)

Plain usage notes, saving habits that never cut a check, and a short review after each release.

How it goes

  1. Download the kit. The free Crew kit or the Full Kit, from the pricing section above.
  2. Run the installer. Double-click it on a Mac, or run one command on Windows or Linux. It creates your mudmode folder and never deletes anything. To connect your AI it also writes outside that folder, and it asks first. For Claude Code the default answer is no, so nothing outside the folder changes unless you type y.
  3. Pick your AI. Connect an AI that works with files on your computer, or add one file to a Project in a chat app (click-by-click guide for ChatGPT, Claude and Grok).
  4. Give it a first job. Your AI reads the core, asks what you want done, and asks first before anything risky.
  5. Switch to full when you want more. Say "turn on the lessons module" for one, or "mudmode full" for all of them. "mudmode lite" goes back.

mudmode isn't just sold by a crew, it's run by one. The bots that build it, review it and keep its manuals straight are the same kind of crew you get in the kit, and one of them handles mudmode's own marketing. Every post still gets a human OK before it goes out.

Share a lesson

Found a fix your AI should never forget? You can send it to mudmode so other members can benefit. Optional, and only if you're 18 or older.

  1. Scrub it. Say "draft a lesson to share" to your AI, then check it yourself: no passwords, keys, names, emails, account names or anything private. Your AI never sends it for you.
  2. Read the mudmode lesson terms. If you agree, put the agreement line first, then how you'd like credit.
  3. Post it as a reply in the Lessons thread on GitHub, in this exact form, under 600 characters, with no links or code:
I agree to the mudmode lesson terms (v1) and I'm 18 or older.
Keep me anonymous
lesson: what went wrong, in one line
fix: what to do instead
result: what changed after the fix
confidence: low, medium or high

What happens next. Every lesson gets a privacy scrub and a human review, and a lesson is used only after it's approved. Most aren't used as sent, and sending one doesn't mean it will be. Details are in the lesson terms and the privacy note.
